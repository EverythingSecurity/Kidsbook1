Mission 1

The Detective's Toolkit 🧰

An argument is not a shouting match or a tantrum. A real argument has two parts:

Premises = the reasons, facts and evidence (the building blocks).
"All dogs have fur. Barnaby is a dog."
Conclusion = what we are trying to prove (the destination).
"Therefore, Barnaby has fur."
Dot says: "A fallacy is a crack in the bridge. The reasons look nice, but they do not really carry the conclusion across."

🎮 Game: Building Block Battles

You need building bricks (or paper cups). One brick is the Conclusion: "Our school should have 15 extra minutes of recess." Find three strong Premise bricks to hold it up: "Moving helps brains focus in math class." "Fresh air improves mood." "Other top schools have longer recess." Then test a sloppy brick like "Because recess is fun!" Does the tower stand or wobble?
Mission 1

Your Turn: Toolkit Practice ✏️

1. Premise or Conclusion?

Write P for premise (a reason) or C for conclusion (the claim).

  1. Birds have wings.  
  2. A penguin is a bird.  
  3. So a penguin has wings.  
  4. Our class should get a pet hamster.  
  5. Hamsters are small and easy to look after.  

2. Strong or Wobbly Block?

Conclusion: "Our class should have a pet fish." Circle the blocks that make a strong tower and cross out the wobbly ones.

A. Fish are quiet and don't need walks.
B. Because fish are cool!
C. Kids who don't want a fish are silly.
D. Looking after a pet teaches responsibility.

3. Does it follow?

Say YES if the conclusion really follows, NO if it is a crack in the bridge.

  1. All cats have tails. Tom is a cat. So Tom has a tail.  
  2. All cats have tails. Rex has a tail. So Rex is a cat.   (Hint: do any dogs have tails?)

Pick a conclusion you care about (like "Pizza is the best lunch" or "We need a later bedtime") and add three strong premises.

Mission 2

Attack and Distraction Monsters 🐉

These monsters do not answer the real question. They throw mud, wave fish or hide behind dummies.

Mud-Slinging Dragon
Ad Hominem
Power: Throws mud at the speaker instead of answering.
Shield: Stick to the Facts: "My spelling grade doesn't change whether my science project works. Let me show you the data!"
Playground example: "Don't listen to Leo's science fair idea. He got a C in spelling last month!"
Smelly Fish Distractor
Red Herring
Power: Drags a smelly fish across the path to lead everyone away from the topic.
Shield: Steer Back on Track: "Sea turtles are cool, but let's get back to the question: is my room clean?"
Playground example: "Did you clean your room?" "Did you know sea turtles swim really fast?!"
Cardboard Dummy
Straw Man
Power: Twists your idea into a silly version, then knocks it down.
Shield: Say What You Really Said: "I never said ban pizza. I said add a salad next to the pizza!"
Playground example: "We should add more salads." "Why do you want to ban all pizza and starve the school?!"
Mirror-Mirror Monster
Tu Quoque
Power: Answers "you did something wrong" with "well, YOU did too!"
Shield: Two Wrongs Don't Fix It: "Maybe I did too, but we are talking about your muddy shoes right now."
Playground example: "You left muddy shoes in the hall." "Well, you left your backpack on the couch yesterday!"
Mission 2

Game and Practice: The Playground Courtroom ⚖️

How to play (3 players): The Prosecutor makes an argument. The Sneaky Defender draws a Fallacy Card (Ad Hominem, Red Herring, Straw Man or Tu Quoque) and answers with that trick. The Logic Judge bangs a (foam!) gavel and shouts: "OBJECTION! Red Herring spotted! Stick to the evidence!" Then swap roles.

1. Name the monster

  1. "Don't trust Mia's picnic plan. She wears silly glasses!"  
  2. "Should we recycle more?" "Did you see the new puppy next door?"  
  3. "I think homework should be shorter." "So you want kids to never learn anything!"  
  4. "Stop interrupting me!" "You interrupt ALL the time!"  

2. Write a polite Logic Shield

Pick two monsters from question 1 and write what you would say to defend the real question.

3. Be the Sneaky Defender

Prosecutor says: "We should have a class vote on the field trip." Write a Red Herring reply that a sneaky defender might use. Then write the Judge's objection.

Dot says: "A shield is never a shout. 'Good point about turtles! Now, back to my room…'"
Mission 3

False Choices and Disaster Slides 🐙

These tricks squeeze you into a corner or scare you with a made-up disaster.

Either-Or Octopus
Bifurcation
Power: Offers only two extreme choices, even when there are many.
Shield: Find the Middle Path: "Or I can play at lunch some days and join the team on others!"
Example: "Either you join our soccer team at lunch, or you are totally against our class!"
Avalanche Slide
Slippery Slope
Power: Turns one tiny step into a giant icy slide to disaster.
Shield: Show Me the Proof Chain: "Why would 5 minutes lead to 5 hours? Let's check each step."
Example: "If teacher gives 5 minutes of homework, next week it'll be 5 hours and we'll never sleep again!"
Double-Trap Dragon
Bogus Dilemma
Power: Says both choices are bad, so you are doomed.
Shield: Grab the Horns or Go Between: "There's a third way: tell the truth and offer to help fix it."
Example: "If I tell the truth about the vase, Mom gets mad. If I lie, I feel guilty. I'm doomed either way!"
Fake Calculator Goblin
False Precision
Power: Uses exact numbers for things nobody can measure.
Shield: Ask Who Measured It: "How did you measure that? Which 87.4%?"
Example: "My new sneakers make me 87.4% faster than anyone in fourth grade!"
Between the Horns! A dilemma is like a bull with two horns. You can go between the horns (find a third option) or grasp the horns (show the bad result will not really happen).
Mission 3

Game and Practice: Between the Horns 🚪

How to play: Draw a card with an Either-Or Octopus statement. Roll a die and find that many third options (or as many as you can in one minute).

1. Name the monster

  1. "Either we study all weekend or we fail the exam!"  
  2. "If I miss one bus, I'll never go to college!"  
  3. "My jokes are 93.7% funnier than yours!"  
  4. "If I say sorry I look weak. If I don't, my friend stays angry. I'm doomed!"

Mission 4

Circular Cats and Force Monsters 🐱

Tail-Chasing Kitten
Begging the Question
Power: Chases its tail: the proof is just the conclusion in other words.
Shield: Find Real Evidence: "That just repeats the claim. What is your actual reason?"
Example: "I should be line leader because I'm the leader, and leaders lead the line!"
Rooster-Sunrise Rooster
Post Hoc
Power: "B happened after A, so A caused B!"
Shield: Look for Other Causes: "Maybe it was studying, not the lucky socks."
Example: "I wore my lucky blue socks and got an A. The socks did it!" (The rooster crows before the sun rises. Does the crow pull up the sun? No!)
Crowd Mob Bandwagon
Ad Numeram
Power: "Lots of people do it, so it must be right!"
Shield: Popular is not Proven: "Many people can be wrong. What is the evidence?"
Example: "Everyone in 5th grade plays this game, so it must be the best ever!"
Playground Bully Rod
Ad Baculum
Power: Uses threats or pressure instead of reasons.
Shield: Reasons, not Threats: "I'll listen to reasons. A threat is not a reason."
Example: "Agree my comic is cooler or you can't sit at our table!"
Correlation is not causation: Two things can happen close together without one causing the other.

Mission 5

Word Chameleons and Sound Tricks 🦎

Word Chameleon
Equivocation
Power: Uses one word with two meanings so the argument sneaks through.
Shield: Ask Which Meaning: "Which 'light' do you mean: not heavy, or not dark?"
Example: "Feathers are light. What is light cannot be dark. So feathers cannot be dark!"
Tangled Grammar Snake
Amphiboly
Power: A sloppy sentence that can mean two things.
Shield: Untangle the Sentence: "Do you mean the desk or the lady has curved legs?"
Example: "For sale: antique desk by an elderly lady with curved legs and brass knobs."
Voice-Stresser
Accent
Power: Changes the meaning by stressing a different word.
Shield: Say It Plainly: "Which word do you want me to stress? Let's say it plainly."

🎤 Voice-Stresser Workshop

Say "We should not eat chocolate now." and stress a new word each time:
  • WE shouldn't eat chocolate now → someone else should!
  • We shouldn't EAT chocolate now → we should drink it!
  • We shouldn't eat CHOCOLATE now → gummy bears are fine!
  • We shouldn't eat chocolate NOW → in five minutes is OK!

🔑 Answer Key and Note for Grown-ups

  • Mission 1: (1) P, P, C, C, P. (2) Strong: A and D. Wobbly: B (just an opinion) and C (an insult, not a reason). (3) YES; NO (a dog has a tail too, so a tail does not prove "cat"). (4) Open-ended: check that the premises really support the conclusion.
  • Mission 2: (1) Ad Hominem, Red Herring, Straw Man, Tu Quoque. (2) Open-ended: a good shield names the real question politely. (3) Open-ended: the reply must change the subject (e.g. "Did you know our class pet turtle…?").
  • Mission 3: (1) Bifurcation (Either-Or Octopus), Slippery Slope, False Precision, Bogus Dilemma. (2) Open-ended, e.g. study 1 hour Saturday, make flash cards, ask for help. (3) Open-ended: the least certain steps are usually the middle ones.
  • Mission 4: Case 1: Post Hoc (the rain is not caused by the hose; weather forecasts are). Case 2: Ad Numeram (lots of buyers do not make candy healthy). Case 3: Post Hoc (skill, practice and luck matter). Matching: Kitten → "That just repeats the claim"; Crowd → "Many can be wrong"; Rod → "A threat is not a reason".
  • Mission 5: (1) "Nothing": first means "no thing", then means "a bad thing is worse than nothing". Equivocation. (2) Either the man or the speaker has the telescope. (3) e.g. "For sale: an elderly lady's antique desk, which has curved legs and brass knobs." (4) Open-ended: I didn't (someone else did); didn't (I borrowed it); take (I only hid it); your (I took someone else's).
  • Mission 6 Blitz: (1) Ad Hominem, (2) Red Herring, (3) Bifurcation, (4) Circulus in Probando, (5) Ad Numeram, (6) Ad Baculum, (7) Straw Man, (8) Equivocation. Shield: e.g. "Lots of kids having phones is not a reason for me to have one. Let's talk about whether I am ready."
For parents and teachers: This book adapts the idea from Madsen Pirie's How to Win Every Argument that logic works as both a shield and a sword. Fallacies are shown as playful monsters across five families: attacks and distractions, false choices, circular and presumption traps, force and pressure, and word tricks. Practise the shields at dinner ("Is that a reason or a trick?"). Remind children that shields are for finding the truth, not for being rude.
