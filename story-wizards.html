Chapter 1

Character: Who is it about? 🧙

A story starts with a character, someone we care about. Characters don't have to be people! They can be animals, toys, food, or even a salt shaker.

The 3 secrets of a great character

  1. A name that's fun to say. Names that start with the same sound are extra catchy: Selena the Salt Shaker, Benny the Brave Bunny, Peter the Purple Penguin.
  2. An age or stage. Is she brand-new? A grandpa turtle? A baby cloud?
  3. 2 or 3 personality traits, like brave, curious, grumpy, shy… and one little flaw! (Selena is brave but afraid of the dark. Flaws make characters feel real.)
🎁 Story Wizard trick: Let your audience help! Ask, "What should we name her?" When friends pick the name, they care about the story even more.

Chapter 2

Setting: Where and when? 🗺️

The setting is the place and time of your story. Story wizards choose between two kinds:

🏠 Logical setting
A place that makes sense for your character. It feels cozy and familiar.
Salt shaker on a kitchen shelf. Fish in the ocean. Kid on a playground.
🎪 Surprising setting
A place that does NOT belong. It makes us ask, "Wait, how did that happen?!"
Salt shaker at the bottom of a toy box. Santa on a tropical beach!
Roary: "Use logical when you want a cozy story. Use surprising when you want a funny or exciting one!"

Problem: Oh no! What now? 😱

Chapter 4

Action: Climb the Story Mountain! ⛰️

The action is how your character solves the problem, step by step. Think of it as climbing a mountain:

  1. Introduction: Meet the character and the setting. "Once upon a time…"
  2. Problem: Something goes wrong!
  3. Attempts and complications: The hero tries a plan… and it doesn't work! So they try something else. (Try at least two things!)
  4. Resolution: Hooray, the problem is solved, through courage, teamwork, or clever thinking!
  5. Takeaway: How has the hero changed? What did they learn?
Roary: "Heroes who win on the first try are boring! Failing is the fun part: it shows our hero never gives up."

Story Time

Selena the Salt Shaker and the Toy Box 📖

Introduction. Selena the Salt Shaker was a brave and curious shaker who lived on the kitchen spice shelf. She was brand-new, and she was afraid of one thing only: the dark.

Problem. One morning, CLUNK! Selena tumbled off the shelf, bounced into a toy box, and slid all the way to the bottom. It was dark. It smelled like crayons and old socks. "Oh no!" cried Selena. "Pizza night is tonight, and the family needs me before dinner!"

First try. "HELP!" she shouted. But the toys were fast asleep, and Selena was very small. Nobody heard her. Then she tried to roll up the slippery wall of the toy box. Whoosh! She slid right back down.

Second try. Selena took a deep breath. "Maybe I can't do this alone," she whispered. She sprinkled a few salt crystals, and they went tinkle-tinkle on a rubber dinosaur's nose. "Achoo!" sneezed Rex the dinosaur. "Who's there?"

"I'm Selena! I'm stuck, and it's too dark to see."

"Don't worry," said Rex. "Pip, turn on your headlights!" Pip the toy car beamed two golden lights, and Selena wasn't afraid anymore. Together, Rex stacked the blocks into stairs, one, two, three, four.

Resolution. Selena climbed the stairs and hopped out just as the family walked into the kitchen. Sprinkle, sprinkle! The pizza was perfect.

Takeaway. That night, Selena sat proudly on the shelf. She had learned that being brave doesn't mean doing everything alone. Sometimes being brave means asking for help.

🕵️ Story detective: Can you spot the 4 secrets? Character = Selena. Setting = a toy box (surprising!). Problem = stuck before dinner. Action = two tries, then teamwork.

Chapter 6

Sparkle Words: Senses, Opposites, and Talking! ✨

1. Wake up the five senses

Don't just say "it was dark." Say "It was dark, and it smelled like crayons and old socks." Sensory words make listeners feel like they're really there!

2. Use opposites

Story wizards love contrast: huge and tiny, brave and scared, happy and sad. Opposites make characters and problems pop. A tiny salt shaker in a giant dark toy box feels exciting!

3. Let your characters talk

Instead of "Selena asked for help," write: "Please, can somebody help me?" whispered Selena. Talking brings a story to life.

Chapter 7

Story Games! 🎲

Stories are best when we play with them. Try these with friends and family!

⭕ Game 1: Story Circle

Sit in a circle. The first person starts with a sentence like "One day, a purple penguin found a map." Each person adds one sentence. Listen carefully so your sentence fits!

🔄 Game 2: Fortunately / Unfortunately

Take turns, switching between good and bad news:
"Fortunately, the bus arrived." → "Unfortunately, it turned into an airplane and flew away!" → "Fortunately, I had a parachute…" This trains your brain to bounce back from surprises!

🎒 Game 3: In This Bag Is a Story

Put 5 random objects in a bag (a spoon, a sock, a key…). Pull them out one at a time and weave each one into the story as you tell it.

🧦 Game 4: Puppet Show

Make a puppet from a sock! Give it a name and a voice. Puppets help shy storytellers feel brave, and they make everyone giggle.

🖼️ Game 5: Storyboard

Draw your story as a comic strip, one picture per step. Seeing the story helps you remember the order!

✏️ Your Turn Draw your story board

Chapter 9

Pitch Like a Pro: Stories That Sell Ideas 💡

Roary: "Grown-ups who run companies use stories to share ideas, and so can you! A pitch is a short, exciting story that tells people about your invention. Let's learn their secret formula."

The SCQA pitch: four steps

Situation: Meet Barnaby the Bear. Every morning, Barnaby loves to pick berries in the sunny forest.
Complication: Uh-oh! His paws get sticky, and thorns poke his claws every time he reaches the high branches!
Question: How can Barnaby pick high berries without getting poked or sticky?
Answer: Introducing the Berry-Grabber 3000! A soft, magnetic claw that reaches the high branches with no thorns and no mess!

Two more copywriter tricks

PAS: Problem, Agitate, Solve
1. Name the problem. 2. Make it feel REALLY annoying. 3. Show the cure!
"Shoes keep coming untied! You trip, your friends giggle, you're late! Zip-Laces never come undone."
BAB: Before, After, Bridge
Show life before, then after, and say your product is the bridge between them.
"Before: soggy cereal. After: crunchy forever. Bridge: the Crunch Cup!"

Chapter 10

Make an Ad: The 4-Panel Commercial 🎬

Ads are mini-stories! Ad makers use the AIDA recipe. Each letter is one comic panel:

PanelWhat goes in it
1. AttentionA big, funny, or surprising problem that grabs eyes
2. InterestYour product shows up: "The Flame-Stopper Toast Shield!"
3. DesireEverything is better! Use sound words and a happy after picture (use contrast: sad and messy before, happy and clean after)
4. ActionTell people what to do: "Buy it! Try it! Available at the Dragon Depot!"

Jingles: ads you can sing!

Advertisers make a jingle, a short rhyming slogan that sticks in your head. "Zap the dirt, erase the grime, Clean-o-Bot works every time!" Add sound effects (stomp for a monster, ring a bell for a bright idea), and use pots, spoons, bells and shakers as instruments.

Chapter 11

The Sales Game: Fortunately / Unfortunately 🤝

Real sellers must answer tricky questions from customers. These are called objections. Instead of giving up, great sellers pivot: they find a clever answer! You already know a game for this: Fortunately / Unfortunately.

How to play
Work in pairs. One is the Seller. The other is the Customer (or use a puppet!). The customer says "Unfortunately…" and the seller answers "Fortunately…". After a few rounds, the customer says "Deal!" and you shake hands.
Seller's secret
Stay friendly, smile, and never argue. Turn every worry into a reason your product is great!

The Inventor's Showcase: Your 60-Second Pitch! ⏱️

It's showtime! You'll present your invention to your family, class, or friends, who are your "investors". You only get one minute!

🔑 Answer Key and Note for Grown-ups

  • Ch.1: Open-ended. Check for an alliterative name, 2–3 traits, and one flaw. Name ideas: Sammy the Squirrel, Dora the Dragon, Marvin the Mouse.
  • Ch.2: 1 L · 2 S · 3 S · 4 L · 5 S. The rest is open-ended.
  • Ch.3: 1 H · 2 S · 3 HS · 4 H (or S) · 5 S. The rest is open-ended.
  • Ch.4: Open-ended. Check that there are two tries and that the first one fails.
  • Ch.5: (1) Top to bottom the numbers are 5, 1, 2, 3, 4. (2) Open-ended, as long as each step follows from the one before. (3) B, M, E.
  • Ch.6: Opposites: giant–tiny, brave–scared, loud–quiet. The rest is open-ended.
  • Ch.8: "What will Rex do next?" = Predict. "Who is Pip?" = Remember. "Have you ever asked a friend for help?" = Connect.
  • Ch.9: (1) Meet Barnaby = Situation · Thorns poke = Complication · How can he reach = Question · Introducing the Berry-Grabber 3000 = Answer. (2) First is BAB, second is PAS. (3 and 4) Open-ended. Check that the pitch has a clear customer, a clear problem, and a named product.
  • Ch.10: (1) Attention, Interest, Desire, Action. (2) Happy dragon with perfect toast = Desire · Sign with price = Action · Crying dragon = Attention. (4) "…every time!" The rest is open-ended.
  • Ch.11: Open-ended. Look for a friendly, clever "Fortunately…" that turns the worry into a benefit. Example: "Fortunately, they have a quiet mode that goes whisper-soft" or "Fortunately, this one lights up so brushing is fun."
  • Ch.12: Open-ended. The plan's times add up to 60 seconds (10 + 15 + 10 + 25).
  • Final test: 1 Action · 2 same (starting) · 3 surprising · 4 two · 5 senses · 6 True · 7 False · 8 Question · 9 Action · 10 Fortunately · 11 jingle.
For parents and teachers: This book follows a simple framework: Character, Setting, Problem and Action, built on a clear beginning, middle and end, with cause and effect between events. Children learn the most when stories are interactive, so use the games, puppets, drawing and questions in the book. Ask open-ended questions (prediction, feelings, connecting to their life) and let your child lead. Praise effort and imagination, not perfection.

Part 2 adapts business-school communication models for children: the SCQA pitch (Situation, Complication, Question, Answer), the AIDA advertising model (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action), and the PAS and BAB copywriting formats. The "Fortunately / Unfortunately" game doubles as practice for answering objections. For a full week, follow the Pitch Week plan (one chapter-activity per day) and finish with the Inventor's Showcase, using the scorecard as encouraging feedback. Everything uses simple materials: paper, props, puppets, and household objects.
